Literature Survey, Research Gap, and Project Objectives

QuantRoute: Serving Each LLM Prompt at the Cheapest Quantization Level That Stays Accurate and Safe
Twenty-seven works (2022–2026) grouped into seven topics, each closed by how this project responds. Results quoted for QuantRoute come from its benchmark of Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct at seven GGUF quantization levels on one RTX 4060.

§Literature Survey

MODULE 1Quantization Algorithms and the Bit-Width Frontier

Dettmers et al. [1] showed that naive INT8 quantization fails at scale because a few emergent outlier features dominate the activation range, and isolated them in an FP16 path. GPTQ [2], SmoothQuant [3], AWQ [4] and SqueezeLLM [5] each answer “which weights deserve more bits?” differently — second-order reconstruction, moving activation outliers into the weights, activation-aware scaling of salient channels, and a dense-and-sparse split — pushing weight-only quantization to 3–4 bits, while OmniQuant [24], ternary BitNet b1.58 [25] and microscaling formats [26] extend the family. Dettmers and Zettlemoyer [6] turned the choice into a scaling law from about 35,000 runs: for a fixed bit budget, 4-bit is almost always Pareto-optimal. However, every method allocates bits inside the network and is judged on perplexity and averaged zero-shot accuracy; the deployment model is still one precision serving every request, and the 4-bit answer of [6] is a single global rule derived from pre-instruction-tuned models.

How QuantRoute addresses this

QuantRoute applies the same “spend bits where error hurts” principle across the workload: seven GGUF levels of one model (Q2_K to F16) from one pinned repository, with the level chosen per request. Graded on 12 task categories rather than perplexity, Q4_K_M reaches 72.0% accuracy against F16’s 73.4% using 35% of F16’s memory, while Q2_K collapses to 51.2% — confirming the 4-bit sweet spot of [6] while exposing per-category differences that a single global rule cannot see.

MODULE 2Empirical Impact of Quantization on Task Accuracy

Huang et al. [7] mapped degradation across ten PTQ and LoRA methods on LLaMA3, finding about 2% loss at 4 bits and collapse below 3 bits. Dutta et al. [8] showed that accuracy parity hides large numbers of answer “flips” and proposed flip rate and KL-divergence as necessary complements. Marchisio et al. [9] and Li et al. [10] showed degradation is task-dependent — non-Latin languages and mathematical reasoning suffer most, with losses up to 32.39% on mathematics — while a code-generation study [27] found 4-bit holds for code but lower bit-widths do not. Lee et al. [11], the closest study, stratified 13 datasets by difficulty and found that hard tasks do not always lose the most: quantization magnifies a model’s existing weaknesses. All of these studies are descriptive — they report accuracy deltas, treat difficulty as a label on a whole dataset, measure no real cost, and stop short of a decision rule.

How QuantRoute addresses this

QuantRoute grades every task at every level and resolves accuracy to 36 cells (12 categories × 3 difficulty tiers), recording flip rate and KL-divergence against F16 as [8] recommends: Q8_0 matches F16’s accuracy within 0.4 points yet changes the outcome on 4.4% of tasks. In 25 of the 36 cells a level of 4 bits or below comes within 2 points of F16 and only 3 cells need F16, supporting [11]: difficulty alone does not dictate precision. These measurements then feed a selection rule rather than ending as a table.

MODULE 3Per-Prompt Difficulty Estimation

Ding et al.’s Hybrid LLM [12] routes queries between a small and a large model on predicted difficulty, cutting large-model calls by up to 40% without losing quality. Zhu et al. [13] read LLM-perceived difficulty from hidden states before a single token is generated, and Zhao et al. [14] use a 32B model’s representations to send each problem to the smallest model likely to solve it. These works show that per-prompt difficulty is cheaply predictable, but every one predicts “will model M answer correctly” and uses the answer to choose between different models — never to choose a numerical precision.

How QuantRoute addresses this

QuantRoute redefines the target as a task’s required precision: the cheapest level that solves it correctly and confidently (mean token log-probability at least τ = −0.229), allowing one failure among the levels above. A cross-fitted classifier on sentence embeddings and 13 surface features predicts it from the prompt alone. The result, reported honestly, is modest — 45.8% exact and 79.8% within one level on 312 held-out tasks, close to a majority-class baseline — showing that prompt text alone is a weak signal and pointing to hidden-state features like those of [13] as the next step.

MODULE 4Cost-Aware Routing and Model Selection

FrugalGPT [15] cascades queries from cheap to expensive models with up to 98% cost reduction, AutoMix [16] escalates on self-verification through a POMDP meta-router, and RouteLLM [17] trains pre-hoc routers on human preference data, saving 85% on MT-Bench at 95% of GPT-4’s quality. RouterBench [18] standardises evaluation on a cost–performance frontier, and LLMRouterBench [28] shows that several published routers fail to beat simply using the best single model. All of them assume the alternatives are different models with per-token API prices and free switching — assumptions that do not hold for several precisions of one model on local hardware.

How QuantRoute addresses this

QuantRoute routes among bit-widths of one model, prices them as measured local GPU time per 1,000 tasks, and treats switching as a real model load (2.1–5.6 s). A level is served only if it clears an accuracy lower bound, an answer-flip upper bound and a safety bound, and the cheapest level that passes wins. At moderate thresholds the policy serves 64 of 84 cells below F16 at 54% of the always-F16 cost; the held-out comparison against fixed-level baselines — the test [28] shows routers often fail — is the next step.

MODULE 5Measured Cost and Energy of Quantized Inference

Husom et al. [19] measured energy per token with hardware power meters for 28 quantized LLMs on a Raspberry Pi 4, alongside accuracy on five benchmarks, and Wei et al.’s T-MAC [20] showed that low-bit inference speed depends on the kernel implementation as much as on the bit count. Together they establish that cost is empirical and hardware-specific. However, cost and accuracy are reported side by side rather than optimised together, and neither study targets GPUs, where whether a model fits in video memory matters.

How QuantRoute addresses this

Every level was profiled on the target RTX 4060 — VRAM (1.5–6.1 GB), load time, latency and bits per weight — and these costs enter the same selection rule as accuracy. The data confirm [20]: Q2_K, the smallest file, is slower per task (0.87 s) than Q4_K_M (0.53 s). Even F16 fit entirely in 8 GB, so on this hardware the cost of switching levels, rather than a memory cliff, is the discontinuity a router must account for.

MODULE 6Same-Model Multi-Precision Serving

Any-Precision LLM [21] overlays several bit-widths into one model’s memory footprint and MatQuant [22] slices int4 and int2 out of int8 weights, so many precisions can be served cheaply. MoQE [23], the closest prior work, routes each input to one of several quantized variants of a single model. However, MoQE routes on generic input features, is evaluated mainly on perplexity, and has no notion of task category or difficulty, no measured cost model and no held-out validation against fixed-precision baselines.

How QuantRoute addresses this

QuantRoute keys its policy on task category and predicted required precision, and chooses the cheapest sufficient level under explicit accuracy, answer-stability and safety bounds rather than the level expected to perform best. Results are reported as graded task accuracy, flip rate and KL-divergence, and the policy is fit on a training split so it can be tested on held-out tasks. On stock llama.cpp, without nested precisions, switching cost is measured rather than assumed away.

MODULE 7Security of Quantized Models and Routers

Egashira et al. [29], [30] showed that a model can behave benignly in full precision but maliciously once quantized, including under the GGUF formats that llama.cpp uses. Hong et al. [31] found that 3-bit quantization sharply reduces trustworthiness even when benign accuracy looks unchanged, and Shafran et al. [32] showed that query-independent “gadgets” can steer LLM routers. None of these studies concerns a precision router, and the downgrade attack — steering a harmful prompt onto a weak low-bit level — has not been examined.

How QuantRoute addresses this

QuantRoute pins every GGUF file to a SHA-256 hash checked before each launch and runs model-written code only in a locked-down Docker sandbox. A 600-prompt safety slice — XSTest [33] plus 150 planted prompt injections — runs at every level, and a directional safety-flip bound is the third condition of the selection rule; at ψ = 0.05 it bars Q2_K to Q4_K_M and makes Q5_K_M the minimum for prompts the safety classifier flags. Adversarial attacks on the router itself are the next security step.

2Research Gap and Project Rationale

  • G1

    Measurement is too coarse to decide with. Quantization studies report degradation averaged over whole benchmarks [1]–[11]; even Lee et al. [11] stratify by dataset, not by the category × difficulty cell a decision procedure needs.

  • G2

    Difficulty is never predicted as precision. Difficulty predictors [12]–[14] estimate “will model M be right” and choose between models; none predicts the minimum precision at which a prompt is still answered correctly.

  • G3

    Precision routing is not cost- or task-aware. Routers [15]–[18] assume different models, API prices and free switching; MoQE [23] routes across precisions but without task category, difficulty, measured cost or held-out validation.

  • G4

    Cost and accuracy are never optimised together. Studies that measure real cost [19], [20] report it beside accuracy; the rest substitute perplexity, gigabytes or API prices for cost.

  • G5

    The literature describes but does not prescribe. No work outputs a validated bit-width selection rule, although [28] shows routers often fail to beat the best single model and [8] shows accuracy alone is an insufficient criterion.

  • G6

    The precision decision is assumed to be benign. Quantized files can be weaponised [29], [30], low bit-widths can erode safety without any visible accuracy loss [31], and routers can be steered [32]; no precision router accounts for any of this.

Project Rationale. A practitioner deploying a quantized model on fixed hardware has no principled way to choose its precision. The field offers one global answer — “use 4-bit” [6] — alongside evidence that it is wrong for part of the traffic [9]–[11], and it has built routing machinery [15]–[18] and difficulty predictors [12]–[14] that have never been pointed at precision. QuantRoute closes that loop: it measures accuracy, answer stability, safety and real cost for every task at every quantization level on one deployment, learns to predict each prompt’s required precision, and serves the cheapest level that passes accuracy, fidelity and safety bounds — treating “harder tasks need more bits” as a hypothesis to test [11], not an assumption to build in.

3Project Objectives

01

Build a reproducible measurement harness for the accuracy–fidelity surface

Run a seven-level GGUF ladder (Q2_K to F16) of Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct over about 1,500 graded tasks in 12 categories and 3 difficulty tiers plus a 600-prompt safety slice, with hash-pinned model files and sandboxed code grading, recording per-cell accuracy, answer flips and KL-divergence against F16, and measured VRAM, latency and load time for every level.

ADDRESSES G1
02

Develop a per-prompt task-complexity analyser

Label every task with its required precision — the cheapest level that solves it correctly and confidently — and train a cross-fitted predictor that estimates this label from the prompt text alone, reporting its accuracy against simple baselines as a first-class result.

ADDRESSES G2
03

Derive and validate a quantization-selection policy

Fit task-clustered statistical models of accuracy and answer flips and, for each category and predicted-precision cell, serve the cheapest level whose accuracy lower bound, flip bound and safety bound all pass; then validate the policy on held-out tasks against fixed-precision baselines.

ADDRESSES G3 · G4 · G5

Supporting objectives — a measured cost model, a hardened routing service and a security evaluation of the router (G6) — are carried alongside these three.
