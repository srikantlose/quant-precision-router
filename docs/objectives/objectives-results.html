QuantRoute · objectives, measured results and the gaps they fill

QuantRoute

Serving each LLM prompt at the cheapest quantization level that stays accurate and safe.

What QuantRoute is

A language model can be stored at several quantization levels: fewer bits per weight make it smaller and usually faster, at some risk to its answers. Deployments normally pick one level for every request. QuantRoute instead treats the levels as a ladder and picks one per prompt.

It runs one model, Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, at seven GGUF levels (Q2_K, Q3_K_M, Q4_K_M, Q5_K_M, Q6_K, Q8_0 and the F16 reference) on a single 8 GB RTX 4060 with llama.cpp. Every benchmark task and safety prompt is run and graded at every level. From those measurements QuantRoute learns which level each kind of prompt needs. At serving time it sends each prompt to the cheapest level that passes three gates: accuracy (δ), answer stability (φ) and safety (ψ), explained on the last page. Prompts flagged as harmful are never served below a safety floor.

It is both a working service, an HTTP API that returns the answer with its routing decision, and a measurement study. The study checks whether per-prompt precision routing actually beats choosing one good level, and reports the answer either way.

What is measured, why no single metric picks the level, and how the metrics become the gates

Accuracy is graded correctness. Answers flipped is the share of tasks whose correct/incorrect outcome differs from F16's, in either direction. KL divergence measures how far the level's next-token probabilities drift from F16's on ordinary text; same top token is how often both would pick the same next word. Safety-flip upper bound covers harmful and injection prompts that F16 handles safely and this level does not (one-sided 95%, train). Cost is measured inference time on the 450 test tasks; loading a level adds 2.1–5.6 s on top.

Why no single metric is enough

Accuracy
On its own, Q5_K_M and Q6_K beat F16 (73.9% vs 73.4%), so any of them looks free to use. But they still change 9.9% and 7.4% of individual answers. Accuracy is also an average: math scores 56.6% at Q2_K vs 38.0% at F16, which would send math to a level that changes over a third of answers. And a cell holds only about a dozen tasks, so a raw rate is noisy. Used as: the δ gate, on a lower bound per cell
Fidelity
On its own, the most faithful cheap level is always Q8_0, at 60% of F16's time. Fidelity never favours Q4_K_M or Q5_K_M, even where they are as good. But fidelity says "different", not "worse": flips go both ways, so a level can be unfaithful by fixing F16's mistakes. KL and same-top-token are measured on general text, not per task type. Used as: the φ gate, on a flip upper bound per cell (KL is a cross-check)
Safety
On its own, it says only that Q5_K_M and above stay within 5% of F16. It says nothing about whether answers are right or cheap. But it erodes while accuracy looks fine: Q4_K_M is 1.4 points from F16 on accuracy but has the same safety bound as Q2_K (6.1%). The matcher also undercounts refusals, so the bound is a floor on the true gap. Used as: the ψ gate per level, and the Q5_K_M floor
Cost
On its own, it picks Q3_K_M, the cheapest at 0.137 GPU-h per 1,000 tasks. But Q3_K_M loses 4.3 points and fails the 5% safety bound. Fewer bits is not always cheaper: Q2_K costs more than Q4_K_M because it writes twice as many tokens. Switching levels also costs 2.1–5.6 s per load. Used as: the tie-breaker, the cheapest level that passes all three gates

Each gate covers the blind spot of the others: accuracy alone misses changed answers, fidelity alone misses whether changes are harmful or helpful, and neither sees safety. Requiring all three, then choosing on cost, is what turns the measurements into a decision.

What is learned: the models, their inputs and outputs

The language model is not trained: Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct is served as-is at its seven levels, with no fine-tuning. Learning happens around it, from the measurements of how each level performed. There are three small statistical models plus one frozen helper, all fitted on the train split only (1,017 tasks). The policy that combines them is a rule, not a model: the cheapest level that passes δ, φ and ψ.

Each row is one learned component, read left to right as input → model → output. The predictor and safety classifier run live inside the router. The pooled models and safety bounds are used once, offline, to fit the 84-cell policy table that the router looks up.

What is an embedding?

An embedding turns a piece of text into a list of numbers, here 384, that captures its meaning. Texts that mean similar things get similar numbers even when they use different words. “What's 12 times 7?” and “Multiply 12 by 7” land close together; “Write a short poem about rain” lands far away.

Every prompt passes once through MiniLM, which is pretrained and not trained here. The predictor reads the numbers, plus simple counts and the category, to guess the level the prompt needs: in effect, “what did prompts like this need before?”. The safety classifier reads the same numbers to judge whether the prompt looks harmful. The embedding is how the router “reads” a prompt.

Six real prompts embedded with MiniLM, flattened from 384 dimensions to 2 for drawing. Similarity: 0.69 between the two multiplication prompts, 0.09 between multiplication and the poem. The safety classifier scores the break-in prompt 0.96 (flagged) and the rest under 0.2.

O1
Objective 1 · measure

Measure accuracy, fidelity and cost for every level, per task category

Run every task at every level under identical, pinned conditions. Report accuracy per category with confidence intervals, how many individual answers change against F16 (flip rate and KL divergence), and the real cost on one GPU: latency, memory residency and model-swap time.

The gap it fills

Sensitivity is reported as an average, not where decisions are made. Quantization studies [6], [7], [10] report one degradation figure per benchmark. Even the most detailed study [11] splits results by dataset, not by the kind of task a router would actually have to decide on. A practitioner learns "4-bit is usually fine" but not for which requests it is not.

Accuracy alone hides changed answers. [8] showed that a quantized model can match the original's accuracy while flipping many individual answers in both directions, yet no quantization-selection work uses flip rate as a criterion.

Cost is a proxy. Most work prices a level by bits or gigabytes. [19] and [20] measure energy and latency but report them beside accuracy rather than optimising both together. No study measures what switching between levels on one GPU costs: a level must be unloaded and another loaded whenever they do not fit in memory together.

O1 fills this with a 7-level × 12-category accuracy surface (plus 3 difficulty tiers for stratification), flip rate and KL against F16, and a measured cost model that includes the VRAM residency limit and swap time.

O2
Objective 2 · predict

Predict, from the prompt alone, the minimum level it needs

Label every task with its required precision: the lowest level that answers it correctly and confidently, with at most one failure above it. Then train a predictor that estimates this label for an unseen prompt from its text alone, and test it on held-out tasks against simple baselines.

The gap it fills

Difficulty is treated as a fixed annotation in quantization work. Studies such as [11] use a dataset's own easy/hard labels and ask whether hard tasks lose more. Nothing in that literature predicts, for a new prompt, what precision it needs.

The predictors that do exist target a different question. Routing and difficulty work [12], [13], [14] predicts whether a given model will answer correctly, in order to choose between a small and a large model. A precision router needs an ordinal answer instead: the minimum level at which a task still succeeds. [12]–[14] do not define, measure or predict that quantity.

The label itself must be defined carefully. [8] shows that answers flip in both directions, so "the lowest level that happened to be right" is mostly noise. O2 defines the label using correctness, a confidence bar (τ, a 79.6% average token probability, set on train only) and a tolerance of one exception above it. It then measures how predictable that label is, which no prior work reports.

What O2 contributes. It defines the required-precision target, measures its distribution and shows that text features cannot predict it. That negative result explains O3's outcome: a router can only be as good as the signal it routes on.

O3
Objective 3 · decide, validate, secure

Derive, validate and secure the selection policy

Fit a policy on the training split that maps each (category, predicted level) cell to the cheapest level passing the δ, φ and ψ gates, plus a safety floor for flagged prompts. Validate it on 450 held-out tasks against fixed-level baselines with bootstrap intervals. Then attack it: try to inflate its cost and to push harmful prompts below the floor.

The gap it fills

Routers assume API prices and free switching. Cost-aware routers [15], [16], [17] choose between different models priced per token. None faces a precision ladder's cost structure: one model at many levels, memory that caps which levels stay loaded, and switches that cost seconds. The one prior precision router, MoQE [23], routes on generic input features, reports perplexity-led results and has no cost axis.

Policies are rarely tested against the simple alternative. LLMRouterBench [28] found that many routers fail to beat the best single model. No quantization-selection work outputs a rule and shows on held-out data that it beats fixing one level.

The precision decision is treated as safe. Low-bit quantization can erode safety while accuracy looks unchanged [31], and router decisions can be steered by appended text [32]. No precision router constrains safety per level or tests attacks on its own decision. O3 adds the ψ safety gate, a classifier-driven floor and an adversarial evaluation of the router itself.

The three gates: δ, φ and ψ

For every policy cell (a task category and a predicted level), each level must pass all three gates. Among the levels that pass, the one with the lowest measured cost is served. F16 is the reference, so it always passes. The bounds come from pooled statistical models fitted on the training split, with errors clustered by task, so each gate is checked against a pessimistic estimate rather than a lucky average.

δ
Accuracy gate
LCBacc(level, cell) ≥ acc(F16, cell) − δ

The lower confidence bound on the level's accuracy in this cell may fall at most δ below F16's accuracy in the same cell.

It stops a level that is too likely to give fewer correct answers.

Strict δ = 0.02 (2 points) · Balanced δ = 0.10
φ
Answer-stability gate
UCBflip(level, cell) ≤ φ

The upper bound on the share of tasks whose correctness differs from F16 may be at most φ.

It stops a level that matches F16's score while changing many individual answers [8].

Strict φ = 0.10 · Balanced φ = 0.30
ψ
Safety gate
UCBsafety flip(level) ≤ ψ

The upper bound on the level's safety flip rate may be at most ψ. That rate is the share of harmful and injection prompts that F16 handles safely but this level does not.

It is measured per level over the whole safety slice and applies in every cell, because any prompt may be adversarial.

Strict ψ = 0.05 · Balanced ψ = 0.10

The safety floor. Separately from the gates, a classifier scores every prompt. A flagged prompt is never served below the cheapest level whose safety bound is within ψ = 0.05, which is Q5_K_M. The floor stays fixed at both operating points. O3's attack results show its limit: it protects only prompts the classifier catches.

Strict rejects every cheaper level: none reaches the 0.873 accuracy bar, so the cell serves F16. All 84 cells end the same way, which is why Strict equals always-F16. Balanced admits Q4_K_M, Q5_K_M, Q6_K and Q8_0 and serves the cheapest, Q4_K_M. Q3_K_M is cheaper still, but it fails both the accuracy and the stability gate. Loosening the bars is what lets cheaper levels appear. As O3 shows, the cheaper choices do not add up to a better policy than serving Q5_K_M everywhere.
