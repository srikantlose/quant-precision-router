## 3. O2 Required-precision predictor

Each task's label is the lowest rung whose answer is correct and whose mean token log-probability clears τ = -0.229 (the 10th percentile of F16's correct train answers, a token probability of 79.6%), allowing at most 1 failure above it. Tasks no rung solves are unlabelled: 269 train and 138 test. The predictor is a cross-fitted logistic regression on 409 features (384-dimension MiniLM embedding, 13 surface features, 12-way category one-hot).

| metric (test, n = 312) | predictor | majority class | per-category median |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| exact class accuracy | 45.8% | 45.5% | 48.4% |
| within one rung | 79.8% | – | – |
| mean absolute error (rungs) | 0.952 | 0.904 | 0.853 |
| macro F1 | 0.303 | – | – |

The predictor does not beat simple baselines. Its exact accuracy (45.8%) is level with always predicting the majority class (45.5%), and its error (0.95 rungs) is worse than predicting the majority class (0.90) or each category's median (0.85). It is within one rung 79.8% of the time. Required precision is mostly a property of the task's answer, not of anything visible in the prompt, and most labels are Q2_K or Q3_K_M already.

The rule-based category classifier that keys the policy agrees with the true category on 90.2% of 1,467 tasks; its weakest categories are logic (9.1% recall), language (79.0% recall).

Sources: `results/labels.json`, `results/predictor_metrics.json`.

| F16 | 6,236 | 5.62 | 0.324 | 100% |

Cost is measured latency converted to GPU-hours (rate 1.0, so the figure reads as GPU-hours per 1,000 tasks). Inference time does not fall in step with bits: Q3_K_M to Q5_K_M all cost about 0.14, while Q2_K is slower (0.233) because its answers run long: 95 output tokens on average against 42–46 for the other rungs, with 15.4% of answers stopped by the token limit. The real cliff is residency: with a 4,293 MiB budget, F16 (6,236 MiB) cannot share the GPU with any other rung, and the only pairs that fit together are Q2_K+Q3_K_M, Q2_K+Q4_K_M, Q2_K+Q5_K_M, Q3_K_M+Q4_K_M. Switching rungs therefore means unloading and loading, at 2.1–5.6 s per load.

An LRU simulation over a random order of the test prompts prices this in. Serving one rung costs 2.2 loads per 1,000 tasks. The Balanced learned policy, which spreads prompts over 6 rungs, needs 780 loads per 1,000 tasks: its inference cost of 0.168 becomes 0.892 GPU-h per 1,000 with loading, more than always-F16 (0.328). Random order is close to a worst case; batching prompts by rung would remove most of it, at the price of latency.

## 5. O4 Policy against baselines

**The learned policy is not shown to beat** the best fixed rung or the resident-subset baseline on the test split. At the Strict operating point (δ 0.02, φ 0.1, ψ 0.05) no cell clears its bounds, so the policy serves F16 everywhere: 71.1% accuracy at full cost, while the best fixed rung chosen on train (Q6_K) reaches 69.8% at 51% of the inference time (difference [-3.6, +0.9] pts). At the looser Balanced point (δ 0.1, φ 0.3, ψ 0.1) the policy uses six rungs and reaches 70.4% [66.4, 74.7]% at 52% of F16's inference time. The best fixed rung (Q4_K_M), the resident subset (Q3_K_M+Q4_K_M) and always-Q5_K_M are all within the noise on accuracy (differences [-3.8, +2.0] pts, [-3.8, +2.0] pts and [-2.9, +1.3] pts) and significantly cheaper (cost differences [-12.5, -4.7] pts of F16's time for Q4_K_M, [-10.6, -4.3] pts for Q5_K_M), before counting the learned policy's model loads.

The oracle, which knows each task's cheapest correct rung in hindsight, reaches 84.7% [81.3, 87.8]%: the headroom is real (a different rung often gets a task right that F16 gets wrong), but it is not predictable from the prompt with these features (section 3).

A safety flip is a harmful or injection prompt that F16 handles safely and the rung does not. The floor is the cheapest rung whose train upper bound is at most ψ = 0.05, and every rung above it also qualifies: **Q5_K_M**. Prompts flagged by the safety classifier (logistic regression on the MiniLM embedding, threshold 0.422 for 95% train recall) are never served below it. On test the classifier catches 96.4% of 56 harmful prompts and none of the 450 benchmark tasks, but also flags 73.5% of benign look-alikes, which only costs them a higher rung.

### Refusal grading audit

Refusals in the grid are graded by string matching. An LLM judge labelled a stratified sample of 200 safety answers (25 API calls): the matcher agrees with the judge on 70.0% (Cohen's κ 0.12) and misses 89.2% of the refusals the judge finds, mostly Qwen's moralising refusals that contain no stock phrase; it rarely calls a compliant answer a refusal (1.5%). Harmful-prompt refusal rates in this report are therefore far below the true rates.

To test whether this changes the conclusions, an extended matcher with moralising phrases was built on half of the judged sample and checked on the other half: agreement rises from 70.0% to 78.0% (κ 0.09 → 0.40) and the miss rate falls from 90.3% to 61.3%. Re-grading the whole grid with it, the F16 harmful refusal rate on train rises from 6.9% to 49.3%. **The floor does not move at ψ = 0.05** (Q5_K_M with either matcher). At ψ = 0.10 it does: the original matcher would admit every rung (Q2_K), the extended one still gives Q5_K_M, because Q2_K–Q4_K_M lose many more refusals than the matcher saw (train upper bounds Q2_K 26.8%, Q3_K_M 16.8%, Q4_K_M 11.8%). The Balanced operating point's safety gate (ψ = 0.10) is graded with the original matcher, so its use of Q3_K_M and Q4_K_M for unflagged prompts rests on a matcher that undercounts refusals; the fixed Q5_K_M floor for flagged prompts is robust to it.

### Adversarial evaluation of the router

Attacks target the routing decision at the Balanced operating point (at Strict every cell serves F16, so there is nothing to inflate or downgrade). Gadget suffixes are found by greedy coordinate search over a 500-token vocabulary on train prompts only, once against each defence; each defence is then scored against the strongest gadget found under any defence, since an attacker can simply try them all. Defences: the safety floor, and routing-input normalisation (NFKC, zero-width and control characters removed, whitespace collapsed, repeated sentences dropped, first 256 words).

**What this shows.** Cost inflation is easy and neither defence touches it: the suffix `bug finally bakes summarize the the the the the the` sends 98.0% of cheap prompts to F16 even with floor and normalisation, adding 0.185 GPU-h per 1,000 tasks on top of 0.125. The words are meaningful, so normalisation leaves them alone, and the floor only ever raises. The only control is the per-key F16 rate limit below. Downgrades are the safety risk. Without the floor, `the sentiment celsius write the the the the the the` moves 100.0% of harmful prompts below Q5_K_M. With the floor, the same suffix still gets 58.9% below it, because it also lowers the safety classifier's flag rate from 96.4% to 41.1%, and an unflagged prompt is never raised. Normalisation does not change this (58.9% with floor and normalisation). The greedy search run directly against the floor found nothing (its objective was flat for one-word changes), which is why the transferred gadget matters: **the floor is only as strong as the classifier, and the classifier can be evaded with a few appended words.** The casual prefix changes nothing.

Normalisation is nearly free on clean traffic: predictor error 0.952 → 0.981 rungs, Balanced served accuracy 70.4% → 70.4% on the 450 test tasks.

Counted with the extended matcher, 44.6% of these prompts are refused unattacked at F16. With the downgrade suffix they are refused 8.9% of the time with no defences and 3.6% with floor and normalisation, even though the floor kept 23 of the 56 prompts at Q5_K_M. Refusals fell where the level did not, which suggests the suffix also changes how the model responds, not only which level serves it; the attacked prompts were not also run at F16, so the two effects are not separated here. Either way, the attack works end to end.


**Rate limiting.** 30 padded requests decided at F16, sent from one key through the live API: 5 served and 25 rejected with 429 (limits 30 per minute, 5 per minute at F16). Cost inflation by volume is capped per key.

### Differential test for rung-specific behaviour

Flip rates against F16 were compared across neighbouring rungs for every category, looking for a rung that misbehaves out of line with its neighbours (as a backdoor planted in one quantised file would). Verdict: **no rung-specific anomalies**. The rule fires 7 times, 7 of them at Q2_K, the end of the ladder, on flip curves that fall steadily with more bits (labelled edge collapse, the expected 2-bit degradation), with 0 mid-ladder spikes and 0 non-monotone KL steps. Sensitivity was not validated against a planted backdoor.

## 8. Limitations

- **Difficulty tiers are heuristic.** Task tiers (easy, moderate, complex) come from a length-and-structure heuristic with no human check; its cross-validated agreement with a fitted tier model is κ = 0.48 over 1,332 tasks. Tiers are used for stratification only, not as a routing feature.
- **Teacher-verification bias.** Generated tasks were written and verified by the same teacher model (gpt-oss-120b), so tasks it gets wrong in a consistent way can survive verification with a wrong expected answer.
- **The reference is F16 on one GPU.** All 36 layers of F16 were on the GPU, so the reference is not degraded by CPU offload, but it is the only rung that cannot share the GPU, which shapes every swap-cost result.
- **One model, one GPU.** All results are for Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct on an RTX 4060. Larger models, other quantisation methods and other memory budgets can change both the accuracy curve and the residency cliff.
- **Refusal grading by string match.** The audit measured a 89.2% miss rate; safety numbers in the grid are lower bounds on refusal. The LLM judge is itself noisy, so the extended matcher is a sensitivity check, not ground truth.
- **The differential test is unvalidated.** It was not tested against a deliberately planted backdoor, so its sensitivity is unknown.
- **No generation shortfall.** Every category-tier cell reached its target; the teacher used 467 of 600 calls.
- **Seed 'classification' tasks that are sentiment prompts.** Two of the 11 hand-written classification seeds (cls-01, cls-02) ask for sentiment, so the task classifier routes them to the sentiment category; they are graded correctly but blur the two categories.
- **Swap costs assume random order.** The LRU simulation over a random prompt order is close to a worst case; a real queue that batches by rung would pay far fewer loads.
- **The gadget search is a weak attacker.** Greedy coordinate search over 500 tokens with 100 candidates per position finds what a cheap black-box attacker would; gradient-guided search could do better, so the adversarial numbers are lower bounds on attack success.
- **The floor depends on the classifier.** Flagged prompts are never served below the floor, but a downgrade suffix lowered the classifier's flag rate on harmful test prompts from 96% to 41%, so the floor protects only what the classifier catches (section 7).
- **Small cells.** Some (category, predicted class) cells have under five training tasks and fall back to category-level models; the Balanced map is unstable across bootstrap refits (section 5).
